Why not a premium estimator?

Published state-average premiums can be off by half or more for any one driver, because price depends on age, driving and claims record, ZIP code, vehicle, limits and (in most states) credit history. A single “your premium is $X” figure tends to anchor you high or low. Comparing several real quotes, including one from an independent broker who can quote regional insurers, is the reliable way to price it.

Reading 25/50/25

Liability limits are written in thousands: bodily injury per person / bodily injury per accident / property damage per accident. 25/50/25 means up to $25,000 for each injured person, $50,000 total for injuries in one accident, and $25,000 for damage to other people's property. State minimums are what's legal to drive, not what protects your savings.

Why 100/300/100 is a common suggestion

An accident with a hospital stay can pass state minimums quickly, and a judgment above your limits can reach your savings and future wages. 100/300/100 covers many serious but not catastrophic accidents. With more to protect, higher limits plus an umbrella policy (often around $150–$300 a year for $1 million) that sits on top of auto and home liability is the usual next step; umbrella insurers typically require 250/500 auto limits underneath.

Collision and comprehensive

A common rule of thumb: once the yearly cost of collision + comprehensive is more than about 10% of the car's value, consider dropping them, especially if you could replace the car from savings. On a $3,000 car with a $1,000 deductible, paying $500 a year protects at most $2,000. A loan or lease usually requires both coverages regardless.

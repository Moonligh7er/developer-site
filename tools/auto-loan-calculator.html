How it works

Auto loans use the same amortization math as any fixed-rate installment loan: M = P · r(1+r)n / ((1+r)n − 1), where r is the APR divided by 12 and n is the number of months (at 0% APR the payment is simply P / n). The part that takes care is P, the amount financed: price + sales tax + financed fees − down payment − trade-in.

Sales tax and trade-ins

In most states sales tax applies to the price minus your trade-in, so a trade-in also saves tax. A few states tax the full price anyway (California, Hawaii and Virginia, for example) and some cap the credit; untick the box above if that's you, and check your state's motor vehicle agency for the exact rule. The down payment is cash you put in, so it reduces the amount financed but not the tax.

Why the APR you get matters more than the sticker

Auto loan APRs vary widely with credit score; the gap between a strong and a weak score can be several percentage points, which on a $25k, 60-month loan is thousands in extra interest. A pre-approval from a credit union or bank before you shop gives you something to compare the dealer's offer against.

The 2025–2028 car loan interest deduction

For tax years 2025 through 2028, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act lets you deduct up to $10,000 a year of interest on a loan taken out after 2024 to buy a new personal-use vehicle whose final assembly was in the US, whether or not you itemize. It phases out above $100,000 of modified AGI ($200,000 joint). This calculator shows interest before any tax saving; see the IRS guidance for who qualifies.

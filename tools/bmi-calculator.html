How it works

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². Imperial inputs are converted exactly (1 lb = 0.45359237 kg, 1 in = 2.54 cm), which is the same as the familiar lb ÷ in² × 703 shortcut to within a hundredth of a point. BMI is rounded to one decimal before it is matched to a category, so the number and the label always agree.

CDC adult categories (age 20+)

  • Below 18.5: Underweight
  • 18.5–24.9: Healthy weight
  • 25.0–29.9: Overweight
  • 30.0–34.9: Class 1 obesity
  • 35.0–39.9: Class 2 obesity
  • 40.0 and above: Class 3 obesity (sometimes called severe obesity)

The WHO uses the same cut-offs with slightly different names (“normal weight” for 18.5–24.9 and “pre-obesity” for 25–29.9). Source: CDC, Adult BMI Categories (checked November 2026).

Real limitations

BMI was designed in the 19th century by Adolphe Quetelet as a population statistic, not an individual health metric. It correlates with body fat at the population level but can be misleading for individuals: athletes with significant muscle mass score high; older adults with sarcopenia (muscle loss) score low despite unhealthy body composition. Better individual metrics include body fat percentage (see the Navy method calculator), waist-to-height ratio, and DEXA scan for clinical accuracy.

Children and teens (2–19) are assessed with age- and sex-specific BMI percentiles rather than these cut-offs. A 2004 WHO expert consultation also suggested lower action points (23 and 27.5) for many Asian populations. This calculator uses the standard adult cut-offs; a healthcare provider can put your number in context.

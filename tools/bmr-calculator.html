BMR by equation

An estimate, not medical advice. Measured resting rates for real people commonly land 10% or more either side of any equation.

How it works: three equations, three answers

Mifflin-St Jeor: 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5 (male) or − 161 (female)
Harris-Benedict, revised: 88.362 + 13.397 × kg + 4.799 × cm − 5.677 × age (male)
  or 447.593 + 9.247 × kg + 3.098 × cm − 4.330 × age (female)
Katch-McArdle: 370 + 21.6 × lean mass (kg), where lean mass = weight × (1 − body fat %)

Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) was fitted on about 500 adults aged 19–78 measured by indirect calorimetry (Mifflin et al., Am J Clin Nutr 51:241). A 2005 systematic review (Frankenfield et al., J Am Diet Assoc 105:775) found it landed within 10% of measured resting rate for more people than the other equations it tested, which is why it is shown first here.

Harris-Benedict dates from 1918; this page uses the 1984 re-fit by Roza and Shizgal (Am J Clin Nutr 40:168). It tends to read a little higher than Mifflin-St Jeor for most adults.

Katch-McArdle uses lean body mass instead of total weight and ignores age and sex, so it needs a body-fat percentage. It can suit very lean or very muscular people better, but it is only as good as the body-fat number you give it.

When the equations disagree

For many adults the three land within about 100 kcal of each other. A bigger spread usually means one equation fits you poorly — for example a lot of muscle (lean-mass equations tend to do better) or age outside the 19–78 range Mifflin-St Jeor was built on. Indirect calorimetry is the way to actually measure it.

The equations were fitted separately on men and women. If neither option describes your body well (for example during hormone therapy), it's reasonable to try both and treat the pair as a range. To turn BMR into a daily calorie figure, use the TDEE calculator.

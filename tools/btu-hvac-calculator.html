Rough sizing

Where the numbers come from

Cooling, official part: the ENERGY STAR room air conditioner chart maps floor area to capacity (for example 150–250 sq ft → 6,000 BTU/hr; 450–550 → 12,000; 1,000–1,200 → 21,000; up to 2,500 sq ft → 34,000), assuming an 8 ft ceiling. Its adjustments: heavily shaded −10%, very sunny +10%, +600 BTU/hr per regular occupant beyond two, +4,000 BTU/hr for a kitchen.

Cooling, rules of thumb added here: capacity scales with ceiling height above 8 ft; insulation shifts it −15% to +20%; climate zone shifts it from +15% (zone 1) to −10% (zones 6–8). These are judgement calls, not ENERGY STAR figures, and the results list each factor used.

Heating: the HVAC trade's rule of thumb of roughly 30–60 BTU/hr per square foot, rising with the climate zone (about 30–35 in zone 1 up to 55–60 in zones 6–8). Good insulation puts you at the low end, poor at the high end. It tends to run high for newer, tight homes.

Why oversizing is a real problem

An oversized air conditioner cools the air fast, reaches the thermostat setting and shuts off before it has pulled out much humidity, so the room feels cold and clammy. It also short-cycles, which wears the compressor and wastes energy. A right-sized unit runs longer, quieter cycles.

Manual J is the actual answer

For central systems and heat pumps, the standard method is ACCA Manual J, which accounts for every window, wall, orientation and air leak. A rough number like this is fine for choosing a window or portable unit. Don't size a central system from it; ask the contractor for a Manual J.

Sources: ENERGY STAR room air conditioner sizing chart; heating range from common HVAC-industry rules of thumb; climate zones from the DOE/ENERGY STAR climate zone map. Checked November 2026.

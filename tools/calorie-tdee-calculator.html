How TDEE is calculated

Two steps. First, basal metabolic rate (BMR) — roughly what your body uses at complete rest — from the Mifflin-St Jeor equation (Mifflin et al., 1990). A 2005 systematic review found it estimated resting rate within 10% for more people than the other common equations:

Male: BMR = 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age + 5
Female: BMR = 10 × kg + 6.25 × cm − 5 × age − 161

Then BMR is multiplied by an activity factor (1.2, 1.375, 1.55, 1.725 or 1.9). These are the conventional multipliers used with BMR equations for decades; they are broad categories rather than measured values, and they are the least precise part of the estimate. To compare equations, see the BMR calculator.

Goals and the safety floor

The weekly-change figure uses the old rule of thumb that 1 lb of body fat is about 3,500 kcal (about 7,700 kcal per kg). It is reasonable for the first few weeks but overstates long-term change, because the body adapts as weight changes; the NIH Body Weight Planner models that adaptation if you want a longer view. Very-low-calorie diets carry real risks (muscle loss, nutrient gaps, gallstones) and belong under medical supervision.

So the target never drops below 1,200 kcal (female) or 1,500 kcal (male), a commonly used floor for unsupervised eating plans. If your goal would land lower, the tool shows the floor and says so. It isn't a hard medical threshold, just a line a general-purpose calculator shouldn't quietly cross.

TDEE numbers are estimates

Real metabolism can differ from the Mifflin-St Jeor prediction by 10% or more, and activity factors add their own error. A practical check is to eat near the estimated maintenance for 2–3 weeks, watch the weekly average of your weigh-ins, and adjust. The number here is a starting hypothesis, not a fixed truth.

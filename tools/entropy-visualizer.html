How It Works

Why this is more random than Math.random()

The plain-language version

When a program calls something like Math.random(), it's not actually rolling dice. It runs a short math formula (xorshift128+ in Chrome, Firefox and Safari) from a hidden starting state. The formula is so simple that a handful of outputs is enough to work out that state, and from there every earlier and later number can be computed.

That sounds abstract until you remember what weak randomness has actually been used to do: predict online poker shuffles, empty crypto wallets whose private keys came from weak generators, and guess “unguessable” password-reset links. For anything where unpredictability matters — passwords, keys, secrets, fair drawings — Math.random() is not safe.

This tool gets its randomness from somewhere different: your operating system’s cryptographic generator, which is seeded from hardware noise, with your own movements stirred in on top. An outsider has no practical way to predict it or replay it.

Imagine you need to pick a number between 1 and a trillion and bet your house on nobody guessing it. With Math.random(), you're essentially saying "I'll roll a die that was pre-rolled in a warehouse last year, and I'll pick the die based on what time I walk in." A sufficiently motivated attacker can walk the warehouse, read all the dice, and check what time you arrived. With physical entropy, you're saying "I'll pick a number based on the exact trajectory a dust mote took in a sunbeam in my kitchen while I was thinking about it." Nobody can replay your kitchen. Nobody can replay the dust mote. And even you can't reproduce it — which is the whole point. Unpredictable to everyone, including the person generating it.

That's the core idea. This tool's "kitchen" is your computer and your hand: hardware interrupt timing and the chip’s built-in random number generator feed the operating system’s pool, and your mouse or finger movements feed a second pool on this page. Your numbers come from a process an attacker can’t practically simulate or replay.

The technical version

Every number this page generates goes through a two-source mixing pipeline. The operating system’s generator does the real work; the motion layer is an extra, an example of what security people call defense in depth.

1
OS Entropy Pool

Your operating system continuously collects entropy from hardware noise — CPU timing jitter, interrupt timing, disk and network event timing, and on modern chips a dedicated hardware RNG like Intel RDRAND or ARM TRNG. Browsers expose this through crypto.getRandomValues(), a Cryptographically Secure PRNG (CSPRNG). It’s the same kind of OS randomness that TLS, password managers and key generation rely on.

2
Mouse & Touch Entropy Pool

As you move your cursor or finger across the canvas, every event feeds the X/Y coordinates and a high-resolution timestamp (browsers round it to somewhere between a few microseconds and a millisecond) into a 256-byte pool via XOR mixing. The exact path is hard to predict for anyone not watching you, though it carries far less entropy than its byte count suggests.

3
SHA-256 Mixing Key

On every mouse event, the tool asynchronously runs crypto.subtle.digest('SHA-256', pool + timestamp + previous key) and stores the 32-byte result as a mixing key. SHA-256 is a one-way cryptographic hash: even if an attacker saw the output, they cannot reverse-engineer the inputs. The key is constantly stirred by your motion.

4
secureFill() — The Actual Generation

When you click a generator button, the tool calls secureFill(buffer). It pulls base randomness from crypto.getRandomValues(), then XORs every byte with the current 32-byte mixing key (repeating the key for longer outputs). XOR-ing in a value that’s independent of the OS output can’t make that output any less random, so the result is at least as unpredictable as crypto.getRandomValues() on its own — even with no motion at all. The protection the other way is partial: if the OS generator were broken, the short, slowly changing key would add some unpredictability, not a full second generator.

5
Key Rotation

Immediately after each generation, the mixing key is XOR-ed with a fresh OS sample, so the same key never masks two separate outputs. (The OS generator already makes each output independent of the last; this just keeps the extra layer from repeating itself.) Every byte secureFill returns also feeds the bit-balance, chi-square and Shannon entropy readouts above.

Verify it yourself

Every piece of this is in the page's source. No external calls, no server roundtrip, no hidden dependencies. Open DevTools, find secureFill, and watch the two-source mixing happen on every click. The readouts under the canvas test every byte the generator has produced: the share of 0 and 1 bits, a chi-square goodness-of-fit test across 16 or 256 bins with its p-value, and the Shannon entropy in bits per symbol (8 is the maximum for bytes). These are basic checks that a badly broken generator would fail; passing them doesn’t prove security, which comes from how the generator is built.

The honest fine print: crypto.getRandomValues() alone is already cryptographically secure for every realistic purpose. The mouse-entropy mixing layer is defense in depth, not a replacement. If the browser's CSPRNG were catastrophically compromised (we know of no such case in a mainstream browser), your mouse movements would still add some unpredictability an attacker can't see. It's belt-and-suspenders engineering, not a workaround for a known flaw.

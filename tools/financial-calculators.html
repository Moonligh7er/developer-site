Compound Interest Calculator

→ Open dedicated page

See how a starting amount and a monthly contribution grow with compound interest. Choose how often interest compounds and whether you add money at the start or end of each month.

Results

How it works

Each month the balance grows by the effective monthly rate for your compounding choice, g = (1 + r/n)n/12, and your contribution is added; a start-of-month contribution also earns that month's growth. At every compounding date this matches the textbook P(1 + r/n)nt, and contributions earn the same effective rate from the month they go in, which is how most calculators handle monthly deposits into an account that compounds less often.

The effective annual rate shows what the compounding is worth: 7% compounded monthly works out to 7.229% a year. The rate is held constant and taxes, fees and inflation are left out, so the result is in future dollars, not today's.

Loan Payment Calculator

Monthly payment, total interest and a yearly amortization summary for a fixed-rate loan. For taxes, insurance, PMI and extra payments, open the dedicated mortgage page.

Results

Amortization summary (yearly)

How it works

Payment M = P · r / (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r is the APR ÷ 12 and n is the number of monthly payments; a 0% loan simply divides the balance by n. As on a lender's schedule, the payment is rounded to the cent, each month's interest is rounded to the cent, and the last payment is adjusted so the balance ends at exactly $0, which is why it can differ from the others by a few cents.

Fixed-rate loans only. Property tax, insurance, PMI and lender fees are not included here; the dedicated mortgage page adds them, along with extra payments.

Results

How it works

Your monthly budget is the sum of the minimums plus the extra amount, and it stays the same until everything is paid. Each month interest is added to every balance (APR ÷ 12), each debt gets its minimum, and the rest of the budget goes to one target debt. When a debt is cleared, its minimum rolls into the next target; that roll-over is what makes both methods work.

Avalanche targets the highest APR first and usually costs the least interest. Snowball targets the smallest balance first and clears whole accounts sooner, which some people find easier to stick with. Minimums are held fixed here; real card minimums shrink as the balance falls and card interest is usually charged daily, so a statement can differ a little.

Savings Goal Calculator

→ Open dedicated page

Work out the monthly amount that reaches a goal by a date, or what a steady monthly amount grows to. Count what you've already saved.

Results

How it works

Deposits go in at the end of each month and the balance compounds monthly at the annual return ÷ 12. To reach a goal G in m months with s already saved, the monthly amount is (G − s(1 + i)m) · i / ((1 + i)m − 1), where i is the monthly rate; at 0% it is what's left divided by the months.

A savings account, a bond fund and a stock fund behave very differently over five years, so treat the steady return as a planning assumption. Taxes, fees and inflation are not included; if the goal is a future price, such as a house deposit or tuition, raise the target to allow for inflation.

Investment Return (ROI) Calculator

→ Open dedicated page

Total return and the annualized return (CAGR) on an investment, from what you put in, what it is worth now, and how long you held it.

Decimals work: 6 months = 0.5.

Results

How it works

ROI = (final − initial) ÷ initial: the total change, however long it took. CAGR = (final ÷ initial)1/years − 1: the steady yearly rate that turns the initial amount into the final one. A 150% gain over five years is a 20.1% CAGR, not 30% a year, because growth compounds.

Both assume one amount in at the start and one value at the end. If you added or withdrew money along the way, or received dividends or rent, fold them into the final value or use a money-weighted return (IRR) instead. Fees and taxes are not deducted.

Tax estimate

Federal bracket breakdown

2026 estimated payments are due April 15, June 15 and September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027. This is an estimate for planning, not tax advice; a tax professional can account for the rest of your situation.

How it works
  • Self-employment tax: 15.3% of 92.35% of net profit: 12.4% Social Security on earnings up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500, plus 2.9% Medicare on all of it. There is no SE tax if that 92.35% figure is under $400.
  • Additional Medicare tax: 0.9% on self-employment earnings above $200,000 ($250,000 joint, $125,000 separate). It is separate from SE tax and not half-deductible.
  • Adjusted gross income: profit minus half of the SE tax.
  • Standard deduction (2026): $16,100 single or married filing separately, $32,200 joint, $24,150 head of household.
  • QBI deduction (§199A): 20% of profit after the SE-tax deduction, capped at 20% of taxable income. Above $201,750 of taxable income ($403,500 joint) it phases out over the next $75,000 ($150,000 joint): in a straight line here, because a solo freelancer usually pays no W-2 wages, and faster for a specified service business, which loses it entirely above that range. From 2026 there is a $400 minimum when your active-business QBI is at least $1,000.
  • Federal income tax: the 2026 rate schedule, 10% to 37%. Below $100,000 of taxable income the IRS tax table works in $50 bands, so a return can differ by a few dollars.
  • State: one approximate rate × (AGI − standard deduction). The list fills in each state's 2026 flat rate, or for graduated-rate states the average rate on $75,000 of single taxable income. Local and city income taxes are not included.
  • Quarterly payments: the yearly total ÷ 4. Paying at least 90% of this year's tax, or 100% of last year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000), in four even parts generally avoids an underpayment penalty.

Assumes this freelance profit is your only income and you take the standard deduction. Not included: W-2 wages (which share the Social Security wage base), credits, itemized deductions, retirement contributions, the self-employed health insurance deduction, the 2025–2028 senior deduction, and AMT.

Figures for tax year 2026, checked November 2026. Sources: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (brackets, standard deduction, QBI thresholds); the Social Security Administration's 2026 wage base announcement; 26 U.S.C. §199A as amended by Pub. L. 119-21; Tax Foundation, state income tax rates and brackets as of January 1, 2026.

FIRE Calculator (Financial Independence)

→ Open dedicated page

Your FI number, how long until you reach it, and the Lean, Fat and Coast variants. Everything is shown in today's dollars, using your return minus inflation.

In retirement, in today's dollars.
Before inflation.
Set to 0 if your return above is already after inflation.

FIRE summary

Year by year

How it works

FI number = yearly spending ÷ withdrawal rate. At 4% that is 25× spending. The 4% figure comes from studies of past US markets over 30-year retirements; many people plan on 3–3.5% for longer ones.

Everything is in today's dollars. The real return is (1 + return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, your yearly savings are assumed to rise with inflation, and the balance grows month by month with savings added each month. Time to FI is the first month the balance reaches the FI number. The future-dollar column adds inflation back, which is closer to what a statement would show.

Coast FI is what you'd need invested today for growth alone to reach the FI number by the age you set. Lean and Fat use 75% and 200% of your spending; they are loose conventions, not rules. Real returns arrive unevenly, and a bad first few years of retirement matter more than the average.

Side hustle breakdown

How it works

Platform fees = revenue × fee %. Profit before tax = revenue − fees − expenses. Tax is set aside on profit only, at your rate. In the US, side income usually owes 15.3% self-employment tax on top of income tax, so 25–35% is a common all-in guess; the freelancer tax estimator can narrow it down.

Hourly rates divide by the hours you enter, so count the unpaid time too. The day-job comparison uses before-tax pay on both sides; it leaves out benefits such as health insurance or a retirement match, which make a salaried hour worth more than its pay rate.

Crypto DCA (Dollar-Cost Averaging) Calculator

→ Open dedicated page

Model buying a fixed dollar amount on a schedule while the price trends from a start price to an end price with swings along the way, and compare it with investing the same total on day one.

Around the trend, 0 to 90. Two full swings over the period.

DCA simulation

Buy history (a smooth model path, not real prices)

A deterministic simulation for learning, not investment advice. Real crypto prices do not follow smooth paths.

How it works

A teaching model, not a forecast. The price moves in a straight line from the start price to the end price with two smooth up-and-down swings of the size you choose. You buy a fixed dollar amount at the start of each period, the fee comes off each buy, and everything is valued at the end price.

Average cost = total spent ÷ coins bought. Because a fixed amount buys more coins when the price is low, this lands below the plain average of the prices. Lump sum puts the same total in on day one at the start price, with one fee. In a market that mostly rises, lump sum tends to come out ahead; DCA mainly limits the regret of bad timing.

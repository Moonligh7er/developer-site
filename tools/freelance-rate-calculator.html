Why your freelance rate needs to be higher than you think

Most new freelancers price themselves the way an employee thinks about salary: "I made $80k at my last job, so I’ll charge $40/hour" ($80k ÷ 2000 hours). That’s the fastest path to burning out at half the income. Real freelance rates have to cover four things a W‑2 employee never sees on their paycheck.

1. The employer half of payroll taxes

As a W‑2 employee, your employer pays 7.65% of your wages toward Social Security and Medicare. As a freelancer, you pay both halves — 15.3% self-employment tax on 92.35% of your net earnings. On $100k of net profit that’s roughly $14,130 before you touch federal income tax. Half of it is deductible, which softens the blow, but the cash still leaves your account.

2. Unbillable hours

A 40-hour workweek doesn’t mean 40 billable hours. Sales calls, proposals, invoicing, admin, learning, marketing, and the gaps between projects all eat into what a client actually pays for. Many freelancers find 60–70% of their hours end up billable. At 70%, a 40-hour week is 28 billable hours — not 40.

3. No employer benefits

Health insurance that an employer largely paid for becomes your bill in full, and individual-market premiums without subsidies often run from several hundred to well over a thousand dollars a month. Retirement contributions have no employer match. No paid time off, no sick leave, no short-term disability, no dental. Every one of those comes out of your billable rate.

4. Overhead that never showed up on your salary

Your employer paid for the software, the laptop, the office, the electricity, the ergonomic chair, the professional licenses, and often the training. As a freelancer, all of it comes off the top. Even a lean solo setup runs $4k–$10k/year.

The rule-of-thumb doesn’t work anymore

You’ll see “multiply your target salary by 2 or 2.5 for your freelance rate” a lot online. A rule of thumb can’t see your health insurance, taxes or billable hours. With the defaults above, the hourly rate comes out near 2.8× the salary-equivalent hourly figure ($80,000 ÷ 2,080 hours), and it moves with your state, health insurance costs, retirement saving and billable hours. The calculator above works it out from the actual numbers instead of a rule of thumb.

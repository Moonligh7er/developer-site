How US federal tax brackets actually work

A common worry: “If I take that raise it'll bump me into a higher bracket and I'll take home less.” That isn't how it works. Federal income tax is marginal: each slice of income is taxed at the rate for its own bracket, not the whole income at your top rate. A single filer in 2026 pays 10% on the first $12,400 of taxable income, 12% on the part from $12,400 to $50,400, 22% from $50,400 to $105,700, and so on. Your marginal rate is the bracket your last dollar sits in; your effective rate is total tax divided by income, which is never higher and usually lower.

Taxable income vs. gross income

Brackets apply to taxable income: gross income minus pre-tax retirement and HSA contributions and other adjustments, then minus either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions. The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 single or married filing separately, $32,200 married filing jointly and $24,150 head of household. People 65 or older (or blind) get an extra $1,650 each ($2,050 if unmarried), and for 2025–2028 there is a further senior deduction of up to $6,000 that phases out at higher incomes. Choosing “gross income” above subtracts only the basic standard deduction.

What this doesn't include

Social Security and Medicare (see the paycheck calculator), state and local income tax, AMT, the lower rates on long-term capital gains and qualified dividends, credits (Child Tax Credit, EITC and others) and the newer deductions for tips, overtime and car loan interest. It is federal ordinary-income tax only; tax software or a preparer will give the full picture.

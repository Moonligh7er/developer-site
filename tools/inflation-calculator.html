Inflation Calculator

Two ways to look at inflation. Historical uses the official US Consumer Price Index (CPI-U) from 1913 to the latest month, so you can see what an amount from one year equals in another. Projection applies a steady rate you choose, for planning ahead.

How it works

Historical: the value in the later year = amount × CPIlater / CPIearlier. Each full year uses the Bureau of Labor Statistics annual average CPI-U (1982–84 = 100). 2026 isn't over yet, so it uses the latest monthly index, September 2026 (336.940). The average yearly rate shown treats each annual average as mid-year, so it is approximate. October 2025 was never collected because of the 2025 federal funding lapse; BLS built the 2025 annual average from the other eleven months.

Projection: prices compound, so after n years at rate r they are (1+r)n times higher. A sum that buys something today needs to be that many times larger in n years, and a future dollar buys 1/(1+r)n of what a dollar buys now.

Historical US inflation

From the 1913 annual average to September 2026, CPI-U rose about 34-fold, an average near 3.2% a year, with wide swings: 13.5% in 1980, slightly negative in 2009, 8.0% in 2022 (annual averages). The Federal Reserve aims for 2% a year over time (measured with a different price index, PCE). For long-range planning, many people use 2.5–3%.

Why 3% matters more than it sounds

At 3% a year, a dollar loses about half its purchasing power in 23–24 years. $100,000 that sits still from age 40 to 64 buys roughly what $49,000 buys today. CPI-U is an average across urban consumers; your own basket (rent, healthcare, tuition) can rise faster or slower.

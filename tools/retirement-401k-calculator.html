How this projection works

Each year your contribution (a percentage of that year's salary, capped at the IRS limit for your age) and your employer's match are paid in monthly, like payroll deductions, and the balance grows at the expected return, compounded monthly. Salary then rises by your raise percentage. The “today's dollars” figure divides the final balance by (1 + inflation)years.

2026 contribution limits

401(k), 403(b), most 457(b): $24,500 of your own deferrals. Age 50 and over: plus $8,000 (total $32,500). Age 60 to 63: plus $11,250 instead (total $35,750). Your contributions plus employer money can't exceed $72,000 (catch-ups sit on top), and only the first $360,000 of salary counts. IRA: $7,500, plus $1,100 at age 50 and over. Source: IRS IR-2025-111 and Notice 2025-67 (November 2025). The calculator grows these limits with your inflation figure in later years; the IRS actually raises them in $500 or $100 steps.

Starting in 2026, if your Social Security wages from the employer were over $150,000 the year before, your catch-up contributions must go in as Roth (after-tax). That changes the tax treatment, not the amount, so this projection is unaffected.

The employer match

A typical formula is “100% of the first 4%” (put in 4% of salary and your employer adds 4%) or “50% of the first 6%” (put in 6%, get 3%). Contributing less than the cap leaves part of the match unclaimed. This calculator treats the match as fully vested.

What the return assumption means

7% nominal is a common planning figure for a stock-heavy portfolio; bond-heavy mixes and target-date funds near retirement usually assume less. Markets don't deliver a smooth 7% a year, so treat the result as one path among many.

What this calculator doesn't include

Investment fees, taxes on withdrawals (traditional) or the up-front tax cost (Roth), vesting schedules, IRA income limits, required minimum distributions, Social Security or other accounts. A fee-only planner can build a plan around the full picture.

How the numbers work Yearly use = bill ÷ price × 12. System kW = use × share to cover ÷ (sun hours × 365 × 0.86 for wiring, inverter and heat losses). Savings = production × electricity price each year, with prices rising and panels losing output at your rates. Ranges: ±15% production, ±10% install cost, and ±1 point of price rise for the 25-year figure. Real results can fall outside them.

Rough estimate based on state averages. Actual production depends on roof pitch and direction (south-facing is best in the US), shade, equipment and your utility's net-metering and time-of-use rules. Savings assume every kWh is worth your full retail price; where exports earn less (California's current net billing, for example) savings will be lower. Bills include fixed charges that solar doesn't remove, so usage is slightly overstated. Inverter replacement and maintenance aren't included. What changed for residential solar in 2026

The 30% federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (IRC §25D) that homeowners used from 2022 to 2025 ended for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act signed on July 4, 2025. In practice: a $25,000 system finished in December 2025 could earn a $7,500 credit; the same system finished in January 2026 or later earns $0 from the federal government, unless new legislation brings the credit back. The credit is claimed for the year the system is installed, not merely bought, and unused credit from a 2025 or earlier installation can still carry forward to later tax years.

A quote that counts a 30% federal credit for a system you buy and install in 2026 is out of date; check the IRS page before signing. Leases and power-purchase agreements are different: the company that owns the panels may still claim a business credit (IRC §48E) for systems that begin construction by July 4, 2026 or are placed in service by the end of 2027, and that can show up as a lower monthly price. This estimator models buying the system outright.

What still applies in 2026

  • State tax credits vary widely. New York, Massachusetts, Maryland, South Carolina, and others have their own credits. Check DSIREUSA.org by state.
  • Utility rebates exist in many service territories, sometimes only for specific equipment or income brackets.
  • Net metering rules determine what you’re paid for excess production. California’s NEM 3.0, for example, dramatically lowered export credits — changing the payback math even for identical hardware.
  • SRECs (Solar Renewable Energy Credits) are tradable in some Northeast/Mid-Atlantic states, adding another income stream.

Why this calculator is a range, not a number

Solar is one of the most assumption-laden purchases a homeowner makes. Roof orientation shifts production ±25%. A single mature oak tree on the south side can drop output 30%. Two identical systems in the same ZIP code can differ 20% in annual output due to microclimate alone. Panel quality varies. Inverter reliability varies. Your utility’s net-metering policy can change during the payback period. Rate inflation is not a straight line.

A single confident number would hide all of that, so this shows a range. Use it as a sanity check: if an installer’s bid falls far outside it, ask why.

  • Electricity prices: EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.B — average residential price by state, January–August 2026 (retrieved November 3, 2026).
  • Federal credit status: IRS Residential Clean Energy Credit page.
  • State/utility incentives: DSIRE database.
  • Install cost reference: Berkeley Lab “Tracking the Sun” and EnergySage marketplace data; the $3.10/W default sits between their recent figures.

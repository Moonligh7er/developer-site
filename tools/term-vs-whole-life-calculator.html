How it works, honestly

Whole life bundles a permanent death benefit with a savings component (cash value) that grows at a guaranteed rate plus any dividends. Term life is insurance only, for a set number of years, with no cash value. The “buy term and invest the difference” idea: term costs far less for the same death benefit, so investing the premium difference may leave you with more money by the end of the term than the whole life policy's cash value.

The math here: each month the term path invests (whole premium − term premium), growing at your return. The whole life path credits your chosen share of each premium to cash value, growing at your cash value rate, unless you enter the illustration figure, which is used instead. Both paths spend the same amount every month. Real cash value is usually near zero for the first few years and then catches up, so a flat share is a simplification; the illustration figure is better.

This only works if the difference really is invested every month. Some people find whole life's forced saving useful for exactly that reason. Two more things favor whole life and aren't in the numbers: cash value grows tax-deferred (taxable-account gains don't), and its death benefit continues after the term ends.

When each product tends to fit

Term: temporary needs (a mortgage, income replacement while children are young), tighter budgets, and anyone who prefers to keep insurance and investing separate. Whole life: permanent needs such as estate liquidity or business buy-sell agreements, people who value the forced-savings structure, and high earners who have already filled other tax-advantaged accounts.

What happens after the term ends

A term policy simply ends. If you still need coverage, you buy new (much more expensive at older ages) or convert to permanent coverage if the policy allows it, often only in the early years. Whole life continues as long as premiums are paid. This comparison stops at the end of the term.

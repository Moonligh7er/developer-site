Daily Water Intake Calculator

A rough daily range of fluids to drink, scaled from body weight and nudged for activity and climate. Individual needs vary widely — thirst and pale-yellow urine are usually better day-to-day guides than any formula. Results update as you type.

Daily fluid range

How it works

The baseline is 30–35 mL of fluid per kg of body weight per day, a common clinical rule of thumb for adults (about 0.46–0.54 fl oz per lb). That range is then multiplied by the activity factor (×1.0 to ×1.5) and the climate factor (×1.0 to ×1.3). Those two factors are this tool's rough adjustments for extra sweat, not values from a study, so treat the top of the range loosely. A cup here is 8 US fl oz (236.6 mL).

Where the “8 glasses a day” rule came from

The familiar “8 × 8 oz glasses” has no strong scientific origin. It's often traced to a 1945 US National Research Council recommendation of about 2.5 litres of water a day — but the same document noted that most of it is contained in prepared food. The “8 glasses” version dropped that qualifier and became folklore.

An evidence-based reference point

The Institute of Medicine (now the National Academies) set adequate intakes in 2004 of about 3.7 litres a day for men and 2.7 litres for women. Those figures are total water from drinks and food; roughly 80% usually comes from drinks (about 3.0 and 2.2 litres) and 20% from food. They describe typical healthy intakes, not a minimum everyone must hit.

Better guides than the calculator

Thirst is a working signal in healthy adults. Urine colour is a quick check: pale yellow is a good sign; dark yellow or amber suggests drinking more. After hard exercise in the heat, weighing yourself before and after helps: each pound lost is about 16 fl oz of water (about 1 litre per kg), and sports-medicine guidance suggests drinking around 1.25–1.5 times that over the next few hours.

When to be careful

Heart failure, kidney disease, SIADH and some medicines call for fluid limits or targets set by a clinician. During very long exercise, drinking large amounts of plain water without electrolytes can cause hyponatremia (low blood sodium), so endurance athletes usually replace salt as well as water. This calculator isn't meant for anyone in these situations.

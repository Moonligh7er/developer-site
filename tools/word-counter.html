Word Counter

Live word, character, sentence and paragraph counts, reading and speaking time, keyword frequency, and Flesch readability scores for English text. Characters are counted the way people see them, so an emoji or an accented letter counts once, and Chinese, Japanese and Thai text is split into words properly where your browser supports it.

Processing happens entirely in your browser. Nothing is uploaded or saved.

How it counts

Words: anything between spaces that contains a letter or a number counts as one word, so “well-known”, “don't” and “3.14” are one word each, and a lone dash or emoji isn't a word. Text in scripts written without spaces (Chinese, Japanese, Thai and similar) is split with your browser's built-in word segmenter (Intl.Segmenter); in older browsers without it, each Chinese or Japanese character counts as one word.

Characters are user-perceived characters (grapheme clusters): 👍🏽 or “é” typed as e + accent count as one, not two or four. “No spaces” leaves out all whitespace, including tabs and line breaks. UTF-8 bytes is the storage size, handy for database and SMS limits. Sentences use the browser's sentence segmenter where available, otherwise a split on . ! ? and their CJK forms; abbreviations such as “Mr.” can still add an extra sentence. Paragraphs are separated by blank lines.

Reading and speaking time

Reading time uses 238 words per minute, the average for silent reading of non-fiction in a 2019 review of 190 studies (Brysbaert); fiction runs a little faster. Speaking time uses 150 words per minute, a typical presentation pace. Technical writing and audiobook narration are usually slower; conversation is faster.

Flesch reading ease and Flesch-Kincaid grade

Flesch Reading Ease = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words ÷ sentences) − 84.6 × (syllables ÷ words). Higher is easier: 90–100 very easy (about 5th grade), 60–70 plain English (8th–9th grade), 30–50 college level, below 30 college graduate. Flesch-Kincaid Grade = 0.39 × (words ÷ sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables ÷ words) − 15.59, roughly the US school grade needed.

Both formulas were built for English. Syllables are estimated from vowel groups with adjustments for silent e and common endings, so treat the scores as a guide; unusual or technical words throw the estimate off. When most of the text isn't in the Latin alphabet, the scores are left out.

Keyword frequency

The ten most frequent words after leaving out common English words (“the”, “a”, “of” and so on) and very short ones, with each word's share of the total word count. Handy for spotting overused words.
